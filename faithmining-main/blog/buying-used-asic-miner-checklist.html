Buying a Used ASIC Miner: A Checklist That Saves You From a Bad Deal

✍️ Chris Johnson — Faith Mining LLC 📅 November 24, 2026 ⏱ 13 min read

A used ASIC is the cheapest way into real hashrate, and the easiest way to get burned. A new S21 XP costs around $4,000. The same machine used sells for about $2,350. An S19 XP that cost thousands new now changes hands for under $300. The discount is real, and so is the risk: a dead hashboard, a swapped model, or a seller who disappears after payment can turn a bargain into a paperweight that still costs you $8 a day in power.

This guide is the checklist to run before you pay. It is not a list of trusted sellers — there is no list that stays current — it is the set of checks that separates a good used machine from a bad one, whatever the listing says.

Run the math before you shop

The machine is only cheap if it earns at your power price. The break-even rule from the hashprice guide still applies: divide hashprice by 24 times the machine’s efficiency, and that is the highest power price the machine can afford. At $27.96 hashprice:

These are the author’s calculations at the stated hashprice, before pool fees, and they assume the machine actually hits its rated hashrate — which is exactly what the checklist below is for. A machine running one dead board earns a third less and the whole table shifts against you.

The payback picture follows the same logic. A used S19 XP at $288 earning $1.04 a day net at $0.04/kWh pays for itself in about 277 days — if it runs at full hashrate the whole time and hashprice holds, neither of which is guaranteed. A used S21 XP at $2,349 earning $1.87 a day at $0.065/kWh takes about 1,256 days, over three years. The cheap machine pays back faster at cheap power; the efficient machine is the one that still earns when power is expensive or hashprice falls further. Buy the machine that fits your power price, not the one with the lowest sticker price.

The checklist: before you pay

Ask for these before money moves. A seller who hesitates on any of them is telling you something.

  1. A test video of this exact unit, running on a pool. Not a screenshot — a video, showing the miner hashing, the pool dashboard, and the serial number label in the same frame. Screenshots are trivially faked; a live video of the unit on a pool is much harder to fake. Ask for the pool to show the worker’s hashrate over at least a few hours, not a single minute.
  2. The dashboard hashboard screen. On an Antminer, the miner status page shows each of the three hashboards separately. You want all three boards detected and each reporting within about 5% of its share of the rated hashrate — about 47 TH/s per board on an S19 XP, about 90 TH/s per board on an S21 XP. A missing board, a board at zero, or a board running far below its share is the number one problem with used miners, and it is the first thing to check.
  3. Kernel logs and the chip map. The web dashboard can be doctored; the kernel log is harder to fake and shows chip-level errors, missing chips, and temperature history. Ask for the log and the chip map that shows how many ASIC chips the firmware sees on each board. A board with dead chips shows up here even when the headline hashrate looks close to normal.
  4. Temperatures and fans at load. ASIC chip temperatures should be under 80°C during normal operation. Ask for the temperature readings while the machine is running at full load, and check that all fans spin freely with RPM readings in spec. Fans are the most common wear item on any ASIC — listen for bearing noise, a grinding or clicking sound, in the test video.
  5. The serial number, checked against the manufacturer. Bitmain runs a warranty check by serial number. Run the serial before you pay: it confirms the model and the manufacture date, and tells you whether any warranty remains. Most used machines are past warranty — Bitmain’s 12-month warranty on S19/S21 series runs from dispatch, and repair centers are in China and Malaysia — but the check also catches the most common swap scam.
  6. The exact model, confirmed in the dashboard — not just the listing title. S19, S19 Pro, S19j Pro, S19k Pro, and S19 XP look nearly identical from the outside and have very different hashrates and efficiencies. The model is printed on the label and shown in the firmware dashboard. Confirm both match the listing, and that the hashrate in the test video matches that model. A relabeled S19j Pro sold as an S19 XP is a $288 machine at a $2,349 price.
  7. What is included, and what is not. Many used listings exclude the power supply. A replacement APW12-style PSU adds roughly $200–$300 to the real cost. Confirm whether the PSU, power cables, and any control board are included, and whether the unit was run on immersion — immersion history needs a re-air kit or an immersion host.
  8. Run time and history. Ask how long it ran, at what temperature, and where. Units with 20,000–30,000+ hours have degraded components and a shorter remaining life. An honest seller tells you; a dishonest one writes “used, tested, working” with no numbers.
  9. The warranty and return terms, in writing. Reputable resellers offer a limited warranty — 14 to 30 days is typical — and a DOA (dead on arrival) window of at least 7 days. “All sales final” from a private seller is not automatically a scam, but it means you are carrying the whole risk, and the price should reflect that.
  10. A landed-cost quote, not just a price. If the unit ships from overseas, ask for DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) pricing to your postal code, so the quote includes duties. If the seller only quotes the machine price, you are the one who finds out about the duty bill at the border.

Paying safely

Where you buy matters as much as what you buy, because it decides what happens when the machine arrives dead.

  • eBay and similar marketplaces give you the eBay Money Back Guarantee and a return window, usually around 30 days, but seller quality varies widely — from legitimate operations offloading fleet hardware to flippers selling untested pallets. Check the seller’s history specifically with mining hardware, not just their overall rating.
  • Specialized mining resellers test, refurbish, and warranty the machines they sell, and can answer technical questions about hashboard configurations and firmware. That support costs more than a private sale; it is worth it on a first purchase.
  • Peer-to-peer and forum deals carry the highest risk and no buyer protection. If you go this route, use an escrow service — Escrow.com is the usual name — and never release funds before you have tested the machine. For orders over $10,000, see or test the miners before releasing funds at all.
  • Never pay by wire transfer or crypto to a seller you cannot verify. Those payments are irreversible. A seller who only accepts wire or crypto, pressures you with “only 2 left” or “price goes up tomorrow,” uses only stock photos, or has no phone number and no verifiable address is running the standard scam playbook. Prices 30–50% below the market medians above are not a deal — miners are commodities with tight pricing, and a price that far below market means the machine is not what the listing says it is.

Shipping and duty: the part the listing leaves out

A full-size ASIC weighs roughly 13–17 kg boxed, and it is heavy freight, not a parcel. Domestic US shipping runs roughly $50–$150 per unit, each way if you have to return it. International air freight from China runs roughly $100–$400 per unit. Budget for both directions: if the machine arrives dead and the seller accepts returns, you are paying to send a 15 kg box back.

Duty is the bigger surprise. ASIC miners enter the US under HTS code 8543.70.9860. One tariff guide dated October 29, 2026 puts the total duty on a Chinese-made unit at about 40.1% — 2.6% general duty, plus 25% under the original Section 301 tariffs, plus a further 12.5% Section 301 layer that took effect July 24, 2026. The $800 de minimis exemption that used to let small parcels in duty-free was suspended for all countries in August 2025, so even a cheap S19 now pays duty. Tariff rates have moved repeatedly through 2026 and are still being litigated, so treat any single figure as a snapshot, not a quote — and get the DDP price from the seller rather than calculating it yourself.

A worked example, using the figures above as estimates: a used S19 XP at the $288 median, shipped from China, could land at roughly $288 + ~$115 duty + ~$150 freight, or about $550 before any PSU — nearly double the headline price. The same machine bought used inside the US at $288 plus $75 domestic shipping lands at about $363. The cheapest listing is not always the cheapest machine.

After it arrives: the first 24 hours

Do not plug it in and walk away. Run it on a pool, watch the dashboard for a full day, and confirm all three boards hold their hashrate and temperatures stay under 80°C. Then reflash to known-good firmware — stock Bitmain firmware or a reputable third-party build — because custom or unknown firmware can hide performance problems or redirect a share of your hashrate to someone else’s wallet. If a board is dead or the hashrate is short, that is what the DOA window is for: document it immediately, with screenshots and the kernel log, and file the claim the same day. eBay and reseller return windows are measured in days, and a dead board found on day 31 is your problem.

One more check for newer machines: since March 2024 Bitmain has shipped Amlogic control boards with a lock that blocks third-party firmware, and stock firmware from September 2025 tightened it further. If you planned to run aftermarket firmware, confirm the control board and stock firmware version before you buy — the firmware guide covers the details.

The bottom line

A used ASIC is a good deal when three things are true: the machine earns at your power price, the hashboards are verified healthy on video and in the logs, and you paid through a channel that gives you recourse if it arrives dead. The S19 XP at roughly $288 and the S21 XP at roughly $2,349 are the current reference points — anything far below those medians deserves suspicion, not excitement. Spend the hour on the checklist. The hour you skip is the one that costs you a hashboard, a duty bill, or a machine that never hashes at all.

Aftermarket Mining Firmware: Braiins OS, VNISH, and LuxOS — Worth It or Not?

✍️ Chris Johnson — Faith Mining LLC 📅 November 20, 2026 ⏱ 15 min read

Every Antminer ships with Bitmain's stock firmware. It works, it is stable, and it runs every chip on a hashboard at the same frequency and voltage, no matter how good or bad that individual chip is. That is the problem aftermarket firmware exists to solve: no two chips are identical. Some can run faster on less power, some need to be held back, and stock firmware treats them all the same.

Braiins OS+, VNISH, and LuxOS all replace the stock system on the control board and tune each chip on its own. The promise is more hashrate, less power, or both. The price is a slice of your hashrate, paid to the firmware developer, plus the warranty and the risk that comes with flashing a machine. This guide works through what each one actually does, what it costs, and when the math works — and when it does not.

What autotuning actually does

Autotuning is the one feature all three share, and it is the whole reason to consider them. The firmware tests each ASIC chip, finds the frequency and voltage that chip handles best, and sets it individually. Strong chips do more work, weak chips do less, and the machine reaches a better ratio of hashrate to watts. Voltage is usually set per power domain — a group of chips sharing a supply — while frequency is set per chip.

That is different from simply overclocking. Overclocking raises everything and hopes the weak chips keep up. Autotuning finds the best point for each chip, which is why the same machine can end up both faster and more efficient than stock, depending on the profile you pick. Most firmware lets you choose: a maximum-hashrate profile, a maximum-efficiency profile that underclocks the machine into its best joules-per-terahash zone, or a power target in watts that the tuner works inside.

How long it takes varies. Published comparison figures put Braiins OS+ at roughly 1 to 9 hours, VNISH at about 3 to 4 hours, and LuxOS at about 1 hour, and the advice is the same for all three: do not judge the result until at least a day has passed, because the firmware is still learning your hardware in the first hours. Treat those times as rough guides, not guarantees — they depend on the model and the profile.

The three firmware, side by side

Braiins OS+

Braiins OS+ is the oldest of the three, from Braiins, the team behind Braiins Pool (formerly Slush Pool). The "+" marks the commercial version with autotuning, as opposed to the free open-source Braiins OS. It is the most established option, with the broadest Antminer coverage: S9, S17, T17, S19 series, and S21 series, with exact model and control-board lists published by the vendor.

Its fee is 2% to 2.5% of hashrate, depending on the model, collected by redirecting that share of hashing power to Braiins Pool. Mining on Braiins Pool itself can rebate the pool fee — the vendor describes a full pool-fee refund when a whole fleet runs Braiins OS — but that is a pool-fee rebate, not a waiver of the firmware fee. Read the firmware terms and the pool terms separately; they are two different charges.

Braiins OS+ is the only one of the three with native Stratum V2 support, which lets a miner build its own block templates instead of taking them from the pool. That matters more to the network than to a single machine's earnings, and it is covered in more detail in the site's upcoming Stratum V2 guide. It also has an immersion mode and a fleet tool, Braiins Farm Monitor, and a newer power-target feature, Price Adapt, that adjusts a machine's operating point as electricity prices and hashprice move.

Braiins publishes its own test figures. For an S21 XP, its published results are 288 TH/s at 12.6 J/TH on default power, against 270 TH/s at 13.5 J/TH on stock firmware, and 268 TH/s at 11.9 J/TH on its efficiency profile. Its published efficiency table shows the same pattern across models: S19j Pro from 30 J/TH stock to 23.5 J/TH, S19 XP from 21.5 to 19.5 J/TH, S21 from 17.5 to 15.4 J/TH, S21 Pro from 15 to 14.1 J/TH. Those are the vendor's numbers, and the vendor adds that results vary — treat them as an upper reference, not a promise for your chips.

VNISH

VNISH is the power user's firmware. It has the longest track record in the custom-firmware space after Braiins, and a reputation in the overclocking and immersion communities for aggressive tuning and granular control: manual per-chip frequency adjustment, voltage curves, immersion-specific profiles, and a built-in dashboard. If Braiins is the automated option, VNISH is the one that hands you the controls.

Its fee is 2% to 2.8% of hashrate, and the exact figure depends on where you get it and which model you run. Vnish.com states 2–2.8%; Vnish.io advertises 1.8–2.8% with negotiated offers for larger miners. Current S19/S21 builds are listed at 2.8%, and Scrypt models L7/L9 at 2%. Confirm the build and the agreement before you flash — there is no single universal VNISH rate. The fee applies on every pool; there is no pool rebate attached to it.

Model support is broad: S9, S17/T17, S19 series, S21 series, T19/T21, and L7/L9, plus some Whatsminer M20/M30/M50 support that changes from release to release. VNISH is closed source, and it does not support Stratum V2 — Stratum V1 only.

One independent benchmark, published by Altair Tech in November 2026 on a new air-cooled S21 XP, found VNISH reaching a low-end efficiency of 12.2 J/TH — about 10% better than stock — and a peak overclock of 319 TH/s. The same test put Braiins OS+ at a peak of 325 TH/s, about 20% over the machine's advertised 270 TH/s. That is one test, on one machine, with a replaced control board to bypass Bitmain's firmware lock, so read it as evidence that both can push an S21 XP well past stock, not as a guarantee of what yours will do.

LuxOS

LuxOS is the newest of the three, from Luxor Technology, the US company behind Luxor Pool and the Hashrate Index. It aims at fleet operators: per-chip autotuning, an autotuner that can switch profiles as hashprice moves, a fleet deployment tool called LuxOS Commander, and enterprise features the other two do not emphasise — SOC 2 Type 2 certification, ultra-fast curtailment (under 5 seconds, down to about 25 W), and a 120V/110V PSU bypass mode for running full-size ASICs on household power.

Its fee is 2.8% of hashrate, the highest flat rate of the three. Mining on Luxor Pool carries a 0% pool fee, which is a pool-fee benefit, not a dev-fee waiver — on Luxor Pool the total is 2.8% plus 0% pool fee; on another pool it is 2.8% plus that pool's fee. Model support is the narrowest: primarily S19 and S21 series, expanding to Whatsminer models. LuxOS is closed source and, in the comparisons reviewed for this guide, Stratum V1 only, with partial Stratum V2 support described as in development in one comparison — check the vendor's current status before counting on it.

What the fee really costs

The dev fee is paid in hash, not in dollars. A share of your machine's hashrate is redirected to the developer's pool, so you keep 97.2% to 98% of the hashrate you produce, depending on the firmware and model. The pool fee is separate and sits on top, unless a rebate or a 0% pool offer applies.

FirmwareDev feePool-fee note
Braiins OS+2–2.5% of hashrate, by modelPool fee rebated on Braiins Pool (conditional, fleet-wide)
VNISH2–2.8% of hashrate, by model and sourceApplies on every pool; no pool rebate

The test is simple: the efficiency gain has to beat the fee. A typical published autotuning improvement of 5% to 25% in J/TH clears a 2% to 2.8% fee comfortably — if you actually get it. On a newer machine that is already close to its silicon limits, the gain is smaller, roughly 3% to 10% on an S21 or S21 XP in the comparisons reviewed, so the margin is thinner and the fee matters more. On an older machine the gain is larger in percentage terms, but that does not mean the machine becomes profitable. It just loses less.

The worked example: an S21 XP at today's hashprice

Take the S21 XP, the current air-cooled flagship from Bitmain: 270 TH/s at 3,645 W, or 13.5 J/TH, on stock firmware. At today's hashprice of about $27.42 per PH/s per day — $0.0274 per TH/s per day — its gross revenue is 270 × $0.0274, about $7.40 a day, before any fee.

It draws 3,645 W, or 87.5 kWh a day. At the $0.11/kWh rate used as a labeled assumption throughout this site — check your own bill — that is $9.62 in power, for a net of about −$2.22 a day before pool fees. At $0.065/kWh the power costs $5.69, for a net of about +$1.72 a day. At $0.08/kWh it costs $7.00, for a net of about +$0.41 a day. Same machine, same hashprice, opposite answer depending on the power price.

Now the Braiins published figures, with the 2.5% dev fee applied as redirected hashrate:

These are the author's calculations at the stated hashprice, using Braiins' published test figures; pool fees are not included, and price and difficulty are frozen, so read them as a snapshot, not a forecast. The pattern is the point: autotuning improves the result at every power price — by about $0.34 a day at $0.11/kWh on default power, and about $0.96 a day on the efficiency profile — but it does not turn a losing machine at residential rates into a winning one. At $0.11/kWh the S21 XP still loses money on power alone, tuned or not. At $0.065/kWh it earns, and tuning adds roughly $0.32 to $0.47 a day on top.

The older-machine case is starker. An S19j Pro at 104 TH/s and 29.5 J/TH stock earns about $2.85 a day gross at today's hashprice and draws about 3,068 W, or 73.6 kWh — $8.10 at $0.11/kWh, a net of about −$5.25 a day. Braiins' published range for that model is 24 to 27 J/TH, which at the same hashrate means roughly 2,500 to 2,800 W, or $6.60 to $7.39 a day in power — a net of roughly −$3.82 to −$4.61 a day after the dev fee. A 20% efficiency gain on a machine that far underwater is still underwater. Firmware cannot rescue a machine whose break-even power price is far below what you pay; it can only narrow the loss.

That is the honest way to use the break-even rule from the hashprice guide: divide hashprice by 24 times the machine's efficiency to get the highest power price it can afford. At $27.42 hashprice, a stock S21 XP breaks even at about $0.085/kWh, and a tuned one at 11.9 J/TH at about $0.096/kWh. A stock S19j Pro breaks even at about $0.039/kWh, and a tuned one at 24 J/TH at about $0.048/kWh. If your power costs more than that number, the machine loses money on electricity alone, no matter how good the firmware is.

The risks: warranty, bricking, and the firmware lock

Warranty. Bitmain's own after-sales policy names unauthorized firmware as a reason cover is refused — clause 4.3.1(2)(j) covers unauthorized changes to firmware or hardware using a third party's products, and (k) covers damage from unauthorized firmware, expressly including firmware that enables an over-frequency setting. In practice, the usual advice is to revert to stock firmware before sending a unit in for service, and most S9, S17, and S19 units in the field are already past their warranty period anyway. But reverting is not a guarantee of coverage, and if your machine is still under warranty, flashing is a real trade: you give up the warranty for the tuning gain. Check how much warranty is left before you decide, not after.

Bricking. Flashing the wrong firmware for your control board, or losing power mid-flash, can leave a miner unable to boot. The good news is that most bricks are recoverable: the SD card (or USB) recovery method brings the machine back in almost all cases, and true hard bricks — where even SD recovery fails — are rare and usually point to a hardware fault, such as a damaged NAND flash chip. The protection is boring and worth doing: identify the exact model, control board, and current firmware version first, back up your settings, and have the recovery image for your board ready before you start. Never flash a machine you cannot afford to have down for a day.

The firmware lock. Since March 2024, Bitmain has shipped Amlogic control boards with a lock that blocks third-party firmware, and stock firmware dated September 2025 or later tightened it further. Depending on the stock build on your machine, installing aftermarket firmware may need a downgrade via SD card first, an unlocking procedure, or a replacement control board — and on the newest stock builds, the standard unlocking methods may not work at all. This is the first thing to check, before the fee or the efficiency table: what control board is in your machine, what stock firmware date is on it, and whether the firmware you want has a published install path for that exact combination. A machine bought new in 2026 is not the same flashing job as a used S19j Pro from 2023.

Reverting. Going back to stock is usually the easy direction — the miner accepts a manufacturer-signed image — but the stock image you restore is usually the newest one on the vendor's portal, and that build may itself refuse third-party installation afterwards. Reverting ends the dev fee and returns the unit to a known state, which is the right call if you are selling it, sending it for service, or no longer trust what is running. Just know that the way back to custom firmware afterwards may be closed.

Who it is for, and who should skip it

Worth a serious look if: you run one or more S19 or S21 series Antminers, you are past warranty, your power price is close to the machine's break-even point so a few percent of efficiency changes the answer, you run immersion or a fleet where per-chip tuning and remote management pay off, or you want Stratum V2 and Braiins OS+ is the only one of the three that offers it natively.

Skip it, or wait, if: your machine is still under warranty and the warranty matters to you, you run a model none of the three supports — that includes the Avalon Nano 3S and Avalon Q in a home setup, and the Goldshell Scrypt miners, which are not Antminers and take none of this firmware — your power price is far above break-even so even a 20% gain leaves you underwater, or your unit has a locked control board and no published install path for your stock build. Stock firmware is free, keeps the warranty intact, and on a new, efficient machine the tuning gain is the smallest of all.

For a home miner with one machine: the gain is real but small in dollars. On the S21 XP example above, tuning is worth roughly $0.34 to $0.96 a day at $0.11/kWh, and only in the sense of losing less — it does not make the machine profitable at that rate. At a cheaper power price it is worth roughly $0.32 to $0.47 a day on top of an already positive margin. Weigh that against an afternoon of flashing, a voided warranty, and a machine that needs a day to settle. For a fleet, the same few percent multiplied across dozens of machines is a different decision entirely.

The bottom line

Aftermarket firmware is a trade, not a free upgrade. You pay 2% to 2.8% of your hashrate and your warranty, and in return you get per-chip autotuning that typically improves efficiency by 5% to 25% — most on old machines, least on new ones — plus, depending on the firmware, Stratum V2, immersion profiles, fleet tools, or fast curtailment. Braiins OS+ is the established choice and the only one with native Stratum V2; VNISH is the granular, power-user choice with the broadest model coverage; LuxOS is the fleet-focused newcomer with the highest flat fee and the narrowest support. None of them makes an unprofitable machine profitable at a high power price. Run the break-even math at your own rate first — hashprice divided by 24 times efficiency — and only flash if the tuned number clears your power price with room to spare.

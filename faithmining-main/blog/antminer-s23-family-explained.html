The Antminer S23 Family, Explained: Bitmain's New Flagship Generation

✍️ Chris Johnson — Faith Mining LLC 📅 November 10, 2026 ⏱ 6 min read

Bitmain's Antminer S23 is the current flagship generation of Bitcoin ASICs, announced May 27, 2025 at the World Digital Mining Summit in Las Vegas and shipping since January 2026. It is the first generation to push efficiency below 10 joules per terahash, and it comes in more variants than any Bitmain generation before it: air, hydro, immersion, and rack-mount versions that range from 318 TH/s to over 1 PH/s in a single unit.

Figures updated November 10, 2026. Market examples in this guide use BTC ~$74,300, network difficulty 148.36 trillion (set October 29, 2026), block reward 3.125 BTC, and network hashrate roughly 990 EH/s. That works out to gross revenue of about $0.0315 per TH/s per day. Market numbers move constantly — re-run your own inputs before acting on them.

That variety is the point of this guide. "The S23" is not one machine. Which one makes sense depends on your power, your space, and your electrical service — and for most home miners, the answer is narrower than the marketing suggests.

What J/TH means, and why it is the number that matters

Efficiency is measured in joules per terahash (J/TH): how much energy it takes to do one trillion hashes. Lower is better, because electricity is the one cost that never stops. A machine at 9.5 J/TH spends roughly a third less on power per unit of work than a machine at 15 J/TH, and that gap is the difference between a machine that survives the next difficulty rise and one that gets switched off.

There is a simple rule for turning efficiency into a decision. Divide the hashprice — the daily revenue per PH/s, currently about $31.50 — by (24 × J/TH) and you get the highest electricity price, in dollars per kWh, at which the machine still covers its own power bill. Above that price, it loses money every day it runs, no matter what you paid for it.

Two things to notice. First, the efficiency leader is the S23 XP Hydro at 8.9 J/TH — the best efficiency Bitmain has ever shipped in a production machine. Second, the Air model is the only one in the family that runs on ordinary single-phase power (220–277V, up to 20A). Every hydro and rack variant needs 380–415V three-phase industrial power, which rules them out of almost every home before the conversation even starts.

One caution on the S23 Air's power figure: most sources, including Bitmain's own spec sheet as carried by AsicProfit and MillionMiner, list 3,498 W, which is consistent with 318 TH/s at 11.0 J/TH. At least one reseller page currently shows 4,129 W for the same model. Confirm the shipped unit's wattage before sizing a circuit or running profitability math — 600 extra watts changes the answer.

The S23 Air at $0.07/kWh earns about $4.13 a day after power. At a $6,899 purchase price that is a payback of roughly 1,670 days, about 4.6 years — and that is before pool fees, repairs, ventilation, and the difficulty rises that will shrink revenue over that span. At the lowest listed price of $4,905, payback is about 1,186 days, still over three years. At $0.11/kWh the same machine earns about $0.78 a day, and payback stretches past a decade. The S23 is a better machine than the S21 Pro it replaces — compare the Air's $4.13/day against the S21 Pro's $1.47/day at the same power price — but "better" and "profitable at your rate" are different claims, and only the second one pays the bill.

The hydro models earn more per day, but only where the infrastructure exists. A 580 TH/s S23 Hydro needs a three-phase supply, a coolant loop, and somewhere to put roughly 18,800 BTU/h of heat. That is a small facility, not a spare room.

How MicroBT answers: the WhatsMiner M66 line

Bitmain is not the only option at this scale. MicroBT's current hydro line is the WhatsMiner M66S++ (356 TH/s, 5,518 W, 15.5 J/TH, best price around $2,959 as of November 10, 2026) and the older M66S (298 TH/s, 5,513 W, 18.5 J/TH, around $1,969).

Run the same math: the M66S++ earns about $11.21 a day and spends $9.27 on power at $0.07/kWh, leaving about $1.94 a day — less than half the S23 Air's daily profit, but on a machine that costs roughly half as much. Its payback at $0.07/kWh is about 1,527 days, comparable to the S23 Air's. Its break-even power price is $0.085/kWh, well below the S23 Air's $0.119/kWh, so it is the first to go underwater when power gets expensive or difficulty rises.

That is the real shape of the choice. The S23 generation wins on efficiency and survives harsher conditions. The M66 line wins on entry price and loses on efficiency. Neither one prints money at residential power rates, and the difference between them is measured in years of payback, not months.

What to check before buying any S23

Verify the spec sheet against Bitmain's current page, not a reseller listing — model names and wattages get mixed up, as the S23 Air's conflicting power figures show. Confirm the cooling format you are actually buying: an "S23 Hydro" without a coolant loop, dry cooler, and three-phase supply is a very expensive paperweight. For the XP Hydro, confirm delivery dates and whether the price is a futures price or in-stock. And check the total delivered cost, including shipping, duties, and taxes — on a machine in this price range, those can add thousands.

For home buyers specifically: the S23 Air is the only model that belongs in the conversation, and even it needs a dedicated 220V circuit, a place to put about 12,000 BTU/h of heat, and tolerance for roughly 75 dB of fan noise. That is garage or outbuilding territory, not a living room. If you do not have that space and that circuit, the honest answer is the same one this site keeps giving: the most profitable home mining decision is often to buy no large miner at all.

The bottom line

The S23 family is a genuine step forward — 8.9 J/TH at the top end, 318 TH/s in a single-phase air-cooled box, and a full range of cooling options for serious operators. At November 2026 numbers, the Air model earns about $4.13 a day after power at $0.07/kWh and about $0.78 a day at $0.11/kWh, with payback measured in years either way. Buy it if you have cheap power, the right circuit, and somewhere to put the heat and noise. If you are missing any of those three, wait — the next generation will be more efficient, and the price of this one will come down.

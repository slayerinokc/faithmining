Read this before you get excited — or discouraged

This page gives a probability estimate, not a prediction and not a promise of earnings. An expected time of, say, 96 years does not mean you will find a block in 96 years. It means that, on average, a miner with that hashrate at that difficulty would find one block in that span — and most miners with those odds will find nothing at all in their lifetime, while a lucky few find one much sooner.

That is the whole story of solo mining for a small miner: the average is long, and the spread around it is enormous. If you need steady income from mining, solo mining is the wrong tool for that job. A regular pool pays small, steady amounts instead. Solo mining is a lottery ticket with a very long wait, not a paycheck.

What solo mining odds actually mean

Bitcoin mining is a guessing game. Your miner makes trillions of guesses per second, and each guess has a tiny chance of being the one that solves a block. The network difficulty sets how hard that guess has to be. Your odds come down to one simple comparison: your guesses per second, against the guesses the whole network would need to make to solve a block at the current difficulty.

1. Expected time to a block

At a given difficulty, the network as a whole expects to need difficulty × 2³² guesses to solve one block. Divide that by your own guesses per second (your hashrate) and you get the expected time, in seconds:

expected time (seconds) = difficulty × 2³² ÷ your hashrate (hashes per second)

The 2³² is not a magic number — it comes from how Bitcoin defines difficulty. Difficulty 1 means a target that takes about 2³² guesses on average, and higher difficulty scales that directly.

2. Probability over a period

Blocks do not arrive on a schedule. Finding one is a random event, so the right question is not "when will I find one?" but "what is the chance I find at least one in this much time?" That chance is:

probability = 1 − exp(−your hashrate × time ÷ (difficulty × 2³²))

In plain language: first work out how many blocks you would expect to find in that period (your hashrate × time, divided by the guesses needed per block). Then the chance of finding at least one is one minus e raised to the negative of that expected number. When the expected number is small, the probability is close to the expected number itself — which is why a 0.0104 expected blocks in a year works out to about a 1.04% chance, not exactly 1.04% but close.

3. Why variance is extreme for small miners

Think of it this way: if your chance in a year is 1%, that does not mean you will find a block after 100 years of trying. Each year is a fresh roll with the same 1% chance. You could find one in your first month. You could also mine for 200 years and find nothing — that outcome is actually more likely than finding one, at those odds. The smaller your share of the network, the more the result is dominated by luck rather than by your average.

That is also why "expected blocks per year" can be a confusing number. An expected 0.01 blocks per year does not mean you get a hundredth of a block each year. Blocks are all or nothing: you get 0 blocks in most years, and a full block (currently 3.125 BTC plus fees) in a rare year. There is no partial credit.

4. What this calculator leaves out

It assumes difficulty stays where you entered it, your miner runs 100% of the time, and your hashrate never drops. In real life, difficulty usually rises over time (which makes odds worse), miners go offline for heat, reboots, and maintenance, and fees on top of the block reward are not counted here. Treat the result as a best-case snapshot at today's difficulty, not a forecast.

Frequently asked questions

Does a higher hashrate change the math, or just the numbers?

Just the numbers. Double your hashrate and you halve your expected time and roughly double your probability over any short period. The formulas stay the same — your hashrate is the only part of the equation you control.

Why is the one-year chance not just 365 times the one-day chance?

Because the chances overlap. You cannot find the same block twice, and the formula counts "at least one block," not "one block per day added up." For small probabilities the difference is tiny, but it grows as the odds get larger — the formula handles it correctly, simple multiplication does not.

Should I solo mine or join a pool?

That depends on what you want. A pool smooths out the luck and pays you regularly for your share of the work. Solo mining keeps the whole block reward if you get lucky, but for most small miners that means long stretches with nothing. Read the guides below before deciding — they walk through the trade-off in plain terms.

Where do I get the current difficulty?

Any Bitcoin block explorer shows it, and this site's other tools use live difficulty. Whatever source you use, enter it here in trillions and double-check the date — difficulty moves every couple of weeks.
